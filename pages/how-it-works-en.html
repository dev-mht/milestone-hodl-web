01 — The mechanism

You buy $MHT. Then you hold. You don't stake, you don't lock, you don't sign anything.

Half the supply — 500,000,000 MHT — sits in a vault inside the contract. Each time the market cap crosses the next $1,000,000 level, the contract releases 5% of what remains in that vault, with a floor of 1M and a cap of 20M MHT.

→ 100% of every release goes to eligible holders, pro-rata. No marketing cut. No founder cut. No split of any kind.

The crossing is proven by a 30-minute on-chain average price, confirmed three times over two hours. The check is a public function: anyone can call it — you, a bot, a stranger. There is no privileged address left in the protocol.

Because the cap binds while the vault is full, the first six milestones release exactly 20,000,000 MHT each.

Nobody has to act for this to work. That is the point.
02 — The number that actually matters

DexScreener and GeckoTerminal will show you a market cap around $2M. The contract reads about $100,000. Both are right. They measure different things.

Aggregators multiply the price by the total supply — all one billion tokens, including the vault, the migration reserve, the burn address, and the tokens still sitting unsold in the pool.

The contract multiplies the price by the eligible supply: only the tokens somebody has actually paid for. Tokens nobody bought are not wealth, and counting them would fire milestones on money that was never spent.

→ At launch, eligible supply was 50,000,000 MHT. The price needed for Milestone 1 is simply $1,000,000 divided by that number.

And here is the part worth understanding: eligible supply grows with every migration and every purchase — so the price required for the next milestone falls on its own, over time.

📌 Excluded from eligible supply: the vault, the migration reserve, the burn address, and the MHT still held by the liquidity pool. Everything else counts. Verifiable at any time by calling getEligibleSupply() and getMarketCap() on the contract.

04 — What changed from V2, and why

The V2 vault never opened. Its release function checked that the caller was an address we had registered — and we registered the wrong one. Chainlink Automation calls through a forwarder, not through the registry we had written down.

The fix was owner-only, and ownership had already been renounced. So the mistake was permanent. The vault worked exactly as written; it just could not be reached.

→ V3 removes the privileged caller entirely. No external service. No subscription. No admin key. No tax. No blacklist. No transfer limit.

Security no longer comes from restricting who may call. It comes from what the function reads. Restricting the caller was never security — it was a dependency, and it is the dependency that killed V2.
